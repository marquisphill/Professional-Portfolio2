Report rationalization for an enterprise OMS modernization

Sr. Business Analyst · AI-assisted analysis · Reporting & data sources

CITY Furniture was retiring a legacy IBM i/AS400 system in favor of IBM Sterling OMS. Everything running off the old system had to be dispositioned before the new one could be scoped — including 1,000+ reports accumulated over years, most of them undocumented and many of them running for nobody.

  • My role: Met with every report owner to begin triage, cutting the inventory to roughly 500. Ran workshops where each owner designated their reports keep or retire with a stated purpose, cadence and owner — down to about 200. Then built an AI-assisted process that analyzed those 200, merged the ones that genuinely belonged together, and returned a QA score for each merge, so human review went to the judgment calls instead of to the whole pile.
  • Challenges: An automated merge is only worth something if you can see what it did with everything it touched. I built the output to carry a coverage manifest — which retained reports actually have a description, a sample and parsed columns — so anything thin surfaced as a visible gap rather than silently dropping out. An evidence trail recorded each report's stated purpose, its parsed columns and its verbatim header, so any conclusion could be walked back to the source.
  • Results: ~200 stakeholder-vetted reports consolidated into ~20 audit-ready data models. Separately mapped those reports back to their real source systems and worked with the dev teams to establish which sources were migrating to the new OMS, which were staying, and with what changes — handing the data team current-state versus future-state sourcing before the build started. Tracked the whole effort on a dashboard and generated the weekly steering report from that data instead of assembling it by hand.
  • Reflection: The consolidation wasn't the hard part. Deciding what a merge is allowed to discard — and being able to prove it discarded nothing else — is what an organization has to trust before it will act on the result.

Logicbroker — replacing the order and inventory system

Business Systems Analyst → Project Manager · Platform replacement · Requirements & delivery

The largest integration project the company had run. Logicbroker didn't sit alongside the legacy order and inventory system — it replaced it outright, and later absorbed the catalog ETL as product enrichment moved in. About 18 months end to end.

  • My role: Drove requirements and coordinated the build across engineers and vendors — not spec-writing handed off to somebody else. Started with 1:1 stakeholder interviews to understand each team's actual process, then brought them together in group sessions to reconcile the workflows against each other. Wrote the BRDs and hundreds of Jira stories with acceptance criteria, led every Agile ceremony on the project, and was business-side owner of the Logicbroker→NetSuite invoice integration as sole liaison to the third-party NetSuite developer.
  • Challenges: Ran a departmental gap analysis against the legacy system — could Customer Service still modify orders, could Accounting still reconcile invoices. Accounting's check surfaced a real hole: Logicbroker had no way to bake per-vendor allowances into invoice reconciliation. Worked with Logicbroker to design a custom feature for it — a table holding each vendor's allowance terms, referenced automatically at invoice generation. Separately, when hundreds of API errors hit in a single day, I traced the root cause to an ambiguous error message on the vendor side, got it rewritten to be actionable, and built a self-service FAQ into the vendor portal so it wouldn't recur at scale.
  • Results: Onboarding cut from six weeks to two, contract to live products. 35+ vendors onboarded on the redesigned workflow. Standardized invoices let NetSuite automate wholesaler and retailer payments. Documented the end-to-end onboarding SOP and drove adoption through real change management — current-state workshop, independent refinement, playback with live edits, then a formal announcement to leadership and the team. Presented status to the CEO monthly and built an executive dashboard surfacing milestones and blockers.
  • Reflection: Managing the vendor relationship mattered as much as managing the build. Leadership wanted a flat two-week onboarding promise; reality ran two weeks to three months depending on each vendor's own integration capability. Working that honestly in both directions got us free development work from Logicbroker and, eventually, their team doing vendor outreach on our behalf.

A queryable, line-traceable requirements layer

Sr. Business Analyst · Internal tooling · Requirements traceability

An enterprise OMS program generates requirements faster than anyone can hold them in their head. Ours lived across hundreds of pages of BRDs spanning 25 workstreams. Every question from a stakeholder or an engineer — did we commit to that, and where — turned into a search task for whichever analyst picked it up.

  • My role: Built a Claude Skill that loads the project's BRDs into a queryable database, so any BA could ask a plain-language question about the requirements and get a concise answer back. Designed it as a reusable Skill rather than a one-off script specifically so it worked for anyone on the team, not just for me.
  • Challenges: What makes a requirements answer usable is being able to defend it. A summary is worthless to a BA who can't tie it back to a signed requirement. So every response carries a unique traceable ID resolving to the source BRD and the exact line the requirement came from — the answer and its receipt arrive together.
  • Results: Answering stakeholder and engineering questions about requirements got noticeably faster, and the answers held up under scrutiny because the source was attached. Related work on the same program used the same approach to synthesize hundreds of pages of BRDs into executive summaries with line-level traceability — about a week's work for something that would otherwise have been a much longer manual read.
  • Reflection: The assumption about AI in analysis work is that it trades rigor for speed. It only does if you let it. Build the traceability in at the start and you get both — and the people who have to sign off stop caring how the answer was produced.

Catalog-as-a-Service (CaaS) and the returns gap

Business Analyst / Project Manager · New revenue · Commercial & accounting

FlexShopper needed new recurring revenue streams and a way to syndicate its product catalog onto merchant partner sites under a revenue-share model — without losing accounting accuracy or falling out of compliance with vendor policies.

  • My role: Led both the business analysis and the project management. Gathered requirements across Product, Sales, Legal, Marketing, Merchandising and Operations, then worked with development and accounting to design the process model and build the automated invoicing system capturing revenue from third-party merchant sites. Also drafted the Statements of Work for our development partners scoping the category microsites the program ran on.
  • Challenges: Returns were the hard part. Revenue implications varied by vendor policy, payment method, which catalog the item came from, who financed it, and the lease origination date. The process had to handle every one of those scenarios and stay scalable across partners without losing accounting accuracy.
  • Results: Reviewing the MSA on my own initiative, I found it had no mechanism to recover revenue on returns for items we had financed out of our own catalog. Took it to the Chief of Merchandising — who had written it — with a diagram showing exactly what the returns coverage was missing. Worked out the scenarios that needed to be written in, settled on a revenue-clawback approach, and drove the system change to support it. Caught before the partner signed.
  • Reflection: Nobody asked me to read the contract. That catch is worth more to me than the invoicing system — the build was scoped work, but the gap was the difference between a clean launch and renegotiating a signed deal.
How I work

Four things I actually do

Less a philosophy than a set of habits that keep showing up in the work.

Decide what to kill

Most analysis effort goes into deciding what to build. The harder question, and usually the more valuable one, is what an organization should stop doing. A thousand reports became twenty data models because somebody was willing to run the retirement conversation with every owner, one at a time.

Automate the pass, audit the output

AI will happily run an analysis pass in an afternoon that used to take a quarter. That's only worth something if you can show what it touched, what it discarded, and where every conclusion came from. I build the coverage manifest and the evidence trail into the process, not after it — because the output has to survive somebody senior asking "how do you know."

Build the tool, not just the document

A BRD answers a question once. A queryable requirements layer answers it for everyone, every time. Same logic behind the dashboards, the onboarding SOP, the vendor-portal FAQ and the intake forms — if I'm doing something by hand twice, I'd rather leave behind something the next person can use.

Find the gap nobody assigned

The prioritization method the team didn't have. The requirements structure that was going to fall apart once it hit development. The clause missing from a contract before anyone signed it. Most of what I've left behind that actually stuck started as something nobody asked me to do.

About

Where I come from, where I'm going

I started on a retail sales floor and worked toward the systems behind it — ecommerce operations, then digital marketing, then catalog and product data, then requirements, integrations and enterprise modernization. That route is the reason I'm useful. I've been the person using the bad process, the person documenting it, and the person replacing it, which makes me hard to sell a solution that only works on a slide.

The pattern I'd point to is that I tend to notice the thing nobody assigned. A prioritization method a team didn't have. A requirements structure that was going to fall apart once it hit development. A compliance conversation that wasn't happening on anything, not just on my projects. A clause missing from a contract before anyone signed it. Most of what I've left behind that actually stuck started that way.

I build my own tools. The ones that worked at CITY Furniture — a Claude Skill giving the BA team line-traceable access to the project's requirements, a consolidation pipeline over a thousand legacy reports, a dashboard that wrote its own weekly steering report — all started as me getting impatient with how long something was taking by hand.

Outside work I produce music, write fiction, and build things that have no business justification whatsoever — including a domain-specific AI framework for exploring how applied AI can support real storytelling. The analytical and the creative have never felt like separate skills to me; they're the same habit of taking something shapeless and giving it structure.

Career goals

I'm looking for senior business analysis and product ownership roles where the problem is genuinely ambiguous — modernization, integration, or a process nobody has been willing to open up.

Near term I'm finishing PSM I to sharpen how I guide teams, on top of the PSPO I I already hold. Longer term I'm moving toward product management — the work I like most is the part where you decide what's worth building, and I'd rather do more of that and less of documenting someone else's decision. I'm especially drawn to media, content and creator tools, where the analytical and the creative sides of the job stop competing.
