Three minutes. You have the numbers and where they came from.

Four things I do.

Decide what to kill

Most analysis work goes into deciding what to build. Deciding what to stop doing is harder and usually worth more. Getting 1,000 reports down to 20 took a retirement conversation with every owner, one at a time.

Set the rules before AI runs

AI gets through an analysis pass in an afternoon that used to take months. What makes the output usable is deciding up front what it has to show you. On the report work I told it what to merge, told it to show its work on every merge, and kept the final call on what actually merged. The artifacts that came back, a coverage manifest and an evidence trail, I kept, adjusted, and used to check the results before the business saw anything.

Which comes out of something else I do-

I would rather build the tool than write the document again.

Build the tool, not just the document

A BRD answers a question once. A searchable version of it answers the same question for everyone. Same reason behind the dashboards, the onboarding SOP, the vendor portal FAQ, and the PIM intake form. If something is taking too long by hand, I would rather leave behind something the next person can use.

Find the gap nobody assigned

A prioritization method the team did not have. A requirements structure that was going to break once it hit development. A clause missing from a contract before anyone signed. A lot of what I have left behind started as something nobody asked for.

Over 1,000 legacy reports cut to about 20 data models

Cut a decade of accumulated reporting down to the models worth rebuilding, with the work behind each decision checkable.

Context

CITY Furniture was retiring a legacy IBM i/AS400 system and moving to IBM Sterling OMS. Everything running off the old system had to be dispositioned before the new one could be scoped. That included over 1,000 reports built up over years, most of them undocumented.

What I did

Met with every report owner to start triage, which cut the list to about 500. Ran workshops where each owner marked their reports keep or retire with a purpose, a cadence and an owner attached. That got it to about 200. From there I set up an AI pass over the remaining reports and gave it the rules it had to work inside: group the ones that belong together, score every merge, show the work, and leave the final decision to me. I reviewed the output and landed on about 20 data models.

Checking the work

The output came back with a coverage manifest showing which retained reports had a description, a sample and parsed columns, plus an evidence trail with each report's stated purpose, parsed columns and original header. I kept both, adjusted them to what the project needed, and used them to check the results. Anything thin showed up as a gap instead of quietly dropping out.

Results

About 200 stakeholder-vetted reports came down to about 20 audit-ready data models. I also mapped those reports back to their source systems and worked with the dev teams to establish which sources were migrating, which were staying, and what was changing, so the data team had current-state and future-state sourcing before the build started. Tracked the whole effort on a dashboard and used AI to generate the weekly steering report off that data instead of building it by hand.

Reflection

The data team got a decided starting point instead of a pile, and every decision behind it was checkable.

The one before it was bigger, and I owned all of it.

Replacing the order and inventory system, end to end

Owned requirements and delivery for the platform that replaced FlexShopper's legacy order system, its inventory management, and eventually its catalog ETL.

Context

The largest integration project the company had run. Logicbroker did not sit alongside the legacy order and inventory system, it replaced it outright, and later absorbed the catalog ETL as product enrichment moved in.

What I did

Drove requirements and coordinated the build across engineers and vendors. Not spec writing handed off to someone else. Started with 1:1 stakeholder interviews to understand each team's process, then brought them together in group sessions to reconcile the workflows. Wrote the BRDs and hundreds of Jira stories with acceptance criteria, led every Agile ceremony on the project, and was business-side owner of the Logicbroker to NetSuite invoice integration as sole liaison to the third-party NetSuite developer.

Challenges

Ran a departmental gap analysis against the legacy system. Could Customer Service still modify orders, could Accounting still reconcile invoices. Accounting's check found a real hole: Logicbroker had no way to bake per-vendor allowances into invoice reconciliation. Worked with Logicbroker to design a custom feature for it, a table holding each vendor's allowance terms, referenced automatically at invoice generation. Separately, when hundreds of API errors hit in one day, I traced the cause to an ambiguous error message on the vendor side, got it rewritten to be actionable, and built a self-service FAQ into the vendor portal so it would not recur at scale.

Results

Onboarding cut from six weeks to two, contract to live products. 35+ vendors onboarded on the redesigned workflow. Standardized invoices let NetSuite automate wholesaler and retailer payments. Documented the end-to-end onboarding SOP and drove adoption through change management: current-state workshop, refinement, playback with live edits, then a formal announcement to leadership and the team. Presented status to the CEO monthly and built an executive dashboard showing milestones and blockers.

Reflection

The vendor relationship mattered as much as the build. Leadership wanted a flat two week onboarding promise and the real range was two weeks to three months depending on each vendor's integration capability. Being straight about that in both directions got us free development work from Logicbroker and eventually their team doing vendor outreach for us.

The smallest thing I built there got used the most.

A searchable requirements layer the whole BA team could use

Built a Claude Skill that let any analyst ask questions of hundreds of pages of BRDs and get answers back with the source line attached.

Context

Requirements for the OMS program lived across hundreds of pages of BRDs spanning 25 workstreams. Every question from a stakeholder or an engineer about what had been committed turned into a search for whichever BA picked it up.

What I did

Built a Claude Skill that loads the project's BRDs into a queryable database, so any BA could ask a plain language question and get an answer back. Built it as a Skill rather than something only I could run, so the rest of the team could use it.

The part that mattered

A summary is not much use to a BA who cannot point to where it came from. Every answer comes back with a traceable ID resolving to the source BRD and the exact line. The answer and the source arrive together.

Results

Requirements questions got answered faster and the answers held up, because the source was attached. The same approach was used on the same program to turn hundreds of pages of BRDs into executive summaries with line-level traceability, done in about a week.

Reflection

Fast answers are easy to get. Answers a BA can defend in a workshop are not. Attaching the source line is what made it usable.

And one that was not in my scope at all.

A new revenue channel, and a gap in the contract behind it

Built the Catalog-as-a-Service revenue-share program, then found the missing revenue-recovery mechanism in the MSA before anyone signed it.

Context

FlexShopper needed new recurring revenue streams and a way to syndicate its product catalog onto merchant partner sites under a revenue-share model, without losing accounting accuracy or falling out of compliance with vendor policies.

What I did

Led both the business analysis and the project management. Gathered requirements across Product, Sales, Legal, Marketing, Merchandising and Operations, then worked with development and accounting to design the process model and build the automated invoicing system capturing revenue from third-party merchant sites. Also drafted the Statements of Work for our development partners scoping the category microsites the program ran on.

Challenges

Returns were the hard part. Revenue implications varied by vendor policy, payment method, which catalog the item came from, who financed it, and the lease origination date. The process had to handle every one of those and stay scalable across partners without losing accounting accuracy.

Results

Reading the MSA on my own, I found it had no mechanism to recover revenue on returns for items we had financed out of our own catalog. Took it to the Chief of Merchandising, who had written it, with a diagram showing what the returns coverage was missing. Worked out the scenarios that needed to be written in, settled on a revenue-clawback approach, and drove the system change to support it. Caught before the partner signed.

Reflection

Reading the MSA was not part of my scope. I read it anyway and found the gap before it cost anything.
